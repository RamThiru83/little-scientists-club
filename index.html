Why does ice cream melt?

Curie's big idea
Heat makes things melt!

Ice cream is made of tiny bits. When it is cold they hold on to each other tightly, so the ice cream is hard: a solid. Warmth from the sun or a hand gives the bits energy, they wiggle apart and slide past each other, and the ice cream becomes a liquid. In the freezer the bits slow down and grab hold again.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for two ice cubes and two small plates.
  2. Put one plate in the sunshine and one in the shade.
  3. Guess which cube will melt first, then check every few minutes.
  4. Talk about it: what gave the ice cube heat?

Curie's big idea
Gravity pulls things down!

The Earth is enormous, and it pulls everything towards itself. We call that pull gravity. It is why a thrown ball always comes back down, why rain falls, and why our feet stay on the ground. Gravity makes big and small things fall together, so a big ball and a small ball dropped at the same time land together.

  3. Watch closely: which one lands first? Try it again to be sure.
  4. Now throw the ball up gently. Which way does it always go?

Curie's big idea
The sun lifts the water up, and the rain brings it down!

The sun warms the sea, and tiny bits of water float up into the air as invisible water vapour. High in the sky it is cold, so the bits huddle into droplets, and millions of droplets make a cloud. The droplets bump and join until they are too heavy to float, then they fall as rain, which runs back to the sea. Round and round: the water cycle.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up

  2. Leave it on the table and wait a few minutes.
  3. Look at the outside of the glass. Where did the little drops come from?
  4. Breathe on a cold window or mirror and watch a tiny cloud appear.

Curie's big idea
A shadow is where the light can't go!

Light travels in straight lines. When it hits your body it stops, because it cannot go through you, so the ground or wall behind you gets no light. That dark patch is your shadow. When the sun is low in the morning or evening, shadows stretch long; at noon, when the sun is high, they are short.

  3. Make a dog, a bird or a rabbit with your hands.
  4. Move your hand closer to the torch. Does the shadow get bigger or smaller?

Curie's big idea
Water pushes up!

When something sits in water it pushes some water out of the way, and the water pushes back up. A small, heavy stone pushes only a little water aside, so the push is too small and it sinks. A wide, hollow boat full of air pushes a lot of water aside, so the water pushes up hard enough to hold it. The same clay sinks as a ball and floats as a boat.

  4. Roll some clay or foil into a ball, then shape it into a boat. Test both!

What makes things happen?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Lots of pieces make it happen!

Things rarely have just one cause. A flower needs soil, a seed, water and sunshine all together, and it takes time. Epidemiologists draw this as a causal pie (Kenneth Rothman's sufficient-component cause model): each slice is a component cause, and the thing happens only when the whole pie is complete. The pieces need not act at the same time: the flower shows once the last piece has done its work (Rothman calls that gap the induction period). Because every slice is needed, taking any one away stops that pie, which is how prevention works: wash the germs off your hands and that tummy ache cannot happen. The same thing can also happen through a different pie (a flower can get its water from rain instead of a watering can), so removing one piece prevents the cases that needed it, not every case. To find out whether something really is a cause, scientists test it by taking it away and seeing whether the result changes. Teddy sat beside the pot all week, but the flower grew just the same without him: being there is not the same as being a cause.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for two clear cups, some cotton wool and two dried bean seeds.
  2. Put cotton wool and a bean in each cup, and stand both cups on a sunny windowsill.
  3. Water one cup a little every day so the wool stays damp. Keep the other cup dry.
  4. Check every day for a week. Which bean grows? Which piece of the pie was missing?

Why do magnets stick?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Magnets pull iron!

A magnet pulls on things that contain iron, such as steel paper clips, iron nails, steel bottle caps and the steel door of many fridges (steel is mostly iron). Things without iron, such as wood, plastic, paper and leaves, are not pulled, and neither are most other metals: aluminium foil, most drink cans, copper and brass are not magnetic. The iron in foods such as spinach or dates is a tiny amount locked inside other substances, so a magnet does not pull them. Coins, keys and spoons vary, which makes them unreliable tests, and some stainless steel does not hold a magnet at all, so a steel paper clip is the surest first test. (Nickel and cobalt are magnetic too, but iron and steel are the ones children meet.) The pull works across a gap, even through paper, but we cannot see it. Every magnet has two ends, called poles (north and south, shown here as red and blue): opposite poles pull together and like poles push apart. Scientists find out which materials are magnetic by testing them one at a time, just as children do in the home experiment. Safety: use a large fridge magnet, and keep small, strong magnets away from young children because they are dangerous if swallowed.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for a large fridge magnet. Magnets never go in your mouth.
  2. Collect a paper clip, a pencil, a leaf and a piece of kitchen foil.
  3. Guess first: will the magnet pull it? Then touch each thing with the flat back of the magnet (the side that sticks to the fridge) and sort them into two piles: pulled and not pulled.
  4. Which things did the magnet pull? Talk about what they have in common. Then try the fridge door!

How do we hear sounds?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Sounds are wiggles!

Every sound starts with a vibration: something moving back and forth very quickly, such as a plucked string, a drum skin or the vocal cords in your throat. The vibrating object pushes on the air next to it, and each little bit of air passes the push on to the next bit, so a sound wave travels through the air to your ear. The air itself does not travel from the string to your ear; each bit only wiggles a little and passes the push along. In the ear, the wave makes the eardrum vibrate too, and the brain hears a sound. Two separate things change a sound. Bigger vibrations are louder (hit the drum harder and the rice jumps higher), and faster vibrations are higher (a short or tightly stretched string vibrates faster than a long or loose one, so it gives a higher note). Plucking the same string harder makes it louder, not higher. Humming with fingers on the throat lets children feel the vibration that makes their own voice.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up to join you. Put your fingers gently on your throat and hum. Can you feel the wiggle?
  2. Ask your grown-up to stretch a rubber band over an open box or a plastic tub.
  3. Pluck the rubber band gently, and keep it away from your face. Watch it wiggle and listen to the sound.
  4. Ask your grown-up to pull the band at the side of the box a little tighter and hold it (not too tight!), then pluck again. Is the sound higher or lower?

Why is it dark at night?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
The Earth spins!

The Earth is a huge ball that spins all the time, making one full turn about every 24 hours. The sun does not switch off, go to sleep or travel round us at night: it keeps shining, but it can only light the half of the Earth that faces it, so that half has day and the half turned away is in shadow and has night. As the Earth turns, each place is carried into the sunlit half (morning), through the day, and out into the shadowed half (evening), so one full turn gives one day and one night. That is also why the sun seems to come up, cross the sky and go down, although it is really the ground under our feet that is turning. When it is midday on one side of the Earth it is midnight on the opposite side: midday in India, for example, is night-time in the Americas. In our pictures we look down from above the North Pole, where the Earth turns counter-clockwise. The pictures are not to scale: the sun is far bigger than the Earth and very far away. The torch-and-ball experiment is a small model: the torch is the sun, the ball is the Earth and the sticker is your home.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for a torch and a ball (an orange works too). Put a small sticker on it: that is your house.
  2. Make the room dark. Your grown-up shines the torch at the ball from a few steps away (never into anyone's eyes), so the whole near half of the ball is lit up.
  3. Turn the ball slowly, like the Earth spinning. Watch your sticker.
  4. When is your house in the light, so it is day? When is it in the dark, so it is night?

Where does the sugar go?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Sugar dissolves in water, but it's still there!

Sugar and salt are made of particles far too small to see, just like the ice cream in Episode 1. When sugar goes into water, the water particles pull the sugar particles away from the crystal, and they spread out evenly between the water particles: the sugar dissolves. We cannot see it any more, but it has not gone: the water tastes sweet, and if salty water is left to dry up, the salt crystals appear again. Dissolving is different from melting. Melting is a solid turning into a liquid when it is heated (Episode 1); dissolving is the particles of a solid spreading out through a liquid. Sugar dissolves even in cold water, just more slowly than in warm water, and stirring speeds it up by bringing fresh water to the crystals. Not everything dissolves: sand and pebbles sink to the bottom and stay there. Only taste with an adult's permission, and never taste the sand water.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for two clear glasses of water, a spoon of sugar, a spoon of sand (or uncooked rice, if you have no sand) and two clean spoons for stirring.
  2. Put the sugar in one glass and the sand in the other. Stir each glass with its own spoon for a minute or two, until you can't see the sugar. Then wash your hands.
  3. Look closely: which one disappears? Which one sinks to the bottom and stays there?
  4. With your grown-up's OK, taste a tiny sip of the sugar water. Is the sugar still there? How do you know? (Never taste the sand water, or the rice water!)

Why do rolling things stop?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Rubbing slows it down!

A rolling or sliding object slows down because of friction, the force that acts where two surfaces rub against each other (air resistance adds a little). Rough or soft surfaces such as carpet, grass and sand produce more friction than smooth, hard ones, and ice produces very little, so the same car rolls much further on ice than on carpet. For wheels this is rolling friction: soft floors such as carpet squash under the wheels, and the axles rub a little. In the Try it activities the car and the box slow down steadily: the rougher the surface, the stronger the pull back, so the sooner they stop. Friction is also useful: it lets shoe soles grip the ground, lets us hold a pencil, and is how bicycle brakes stop the wheels. Letting the same car roll from the same place on different surfaces, as in the home experiment, is a fair test: only the surface changes, so any difference in distance comes from the surface.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up to help you make a ramp: lean a big book against a cushion or a box.
  2. Let a toy car roll down onto a smooth floor. Put a block where it stops.
  3. Now spread a towel at the bottom of the ramp and roll the car again from the same place. Mark where it stops.
  4. Where did it go further? Which floor rubbed more?

Where do rainbows come from?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Raindrops split the sunlight!

Sunlight looks white, but it is a mixture of all the colours. When light passes from air into water or glass it bends (refraction), and each colour bends by a slightly different amount: violet bends the most and red the least. That is why a glass prism spreads white light into a band of colours. A raindrop works in the same way: sunlight enters the drop and bends, reflects off the back of the drop, and bends again as it leaves, so the colours fan out. From each drop only one colour reaches your eye, so red reaches you from drops higher in the sky and violet from lower ones, which puts red on the outside of the bow and violet on the inside. You see a rainbow only with the sun behind you and rain in front of you, and it is easiest to see when the sun is low, in the morning or late afternoon. A rainbow is really a full circle around the point directly opposite the sun; the ground usually hides the lower part. Because it is made by whichever drops sit at the right angle from your own eyes, the rainbow moves when you move, you can never reach its end, and everyone sees their own rainbow. In the video the bending of the colours is drawn much bigger than it really is, so that it can be seen. More than 350 years ago, scientists used prisms to show that white sunlight is a mixture of colours. Sources: US National Weather Service, Flagstaff office ("Rainbows"); Australian Bureau of Meteorology ("How do rainbows form?").

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for a spray bottle of water on a sunny morning or late afternoon, when the sun is low.
  2. Go outside and stand with the sun behind you, so your shadow points away from the sun. Never look straight at the sun.
  3. Spray a fine mist in front of you and slowly move the spray up, down and around until the colours appear.
  4. Can you make your own rainbow? Which colour is on the outside, and which is on the inside?

Why does the Moon change shape?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
The Moon stays round, our view changes!

The Moon does not make its own light: moonlight is sunlight reflected off the Moon's surface. The Sun always lights up one half of the Moon (its day side), while the other half is dark (its night side). The Moon travels around the Earth in its orbit, and as it goes around we see different amounts of the sunlit half. When the dark side faces us we cannot see the Moon at all: a new Moon. Then the lit part we can see grows (waxing): a thin crescent, a half (first quarter), a gibbous Moon and the full Moon, when the whole sunlit side faces the Earth. Then it shrinks again (waning) through a half (third quarter) and a crescent back to a new Moon. From the northern half of the Earth, including India, the growing Moon is bright on the right and the shrinking Moon bright on the left. The Moon's shape never changes; only our view of its sunlit half does. The cycle from one new Moon to the next takes about 29.5 days (one trip around the Earth takes about 27.3 days). The Moon can often be seen in the daytime, too. Sources: NASA Space Place ("What Are the Moon's Phases?") and NASA Science ("Moon Phases").

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for a lamp with its shade taken off, and a ball (an orange works too), and make the room dark.
  2. The grown-up puts the lamp on a table at one side of the room. Don't touch the bulb (it can get warm), and don't look straight into the light.
  3. Clear a space so you don't bump into things. Hold the ball up at arm's length, a little above your head, and turn slowly to your left in a full circle, watching the ball all the time.
  4. When is the ball dark, half bright, or all bright? Can you make a new Moon, a crescent and a full Moon?

How do we know what made something happen?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Ask what would happen without it!

To know whether something caused an outcome, we need to compare what actually happened with what would have happened if, contrary to fact, that thing had been different. This 'what would have happened' is called the counterfactual (or potential) outcome. The ideal comparison is a thought experiment: the same people, over the same time, both with and without the exposure, with nothing else different. Rothman calls this the counterfactual ideal. It can never be achieved, because each person lives through only one of the two conditions: we observe one outcome and the other stays unknown. So, in practice, scientists substitute the experience of a real comparison group without the exposure, and the key question becomes whether that group really shows what would have happened to the exposed group. When it does not, the comparison is confounded. Study designs try to make the groups comparable: random assignment (as in trials of new medicines) tends to balance other characteristics when there are enough people; crossover studies compare people with themselves at different times; observational studies choose comparison groups with similar risk factors. Even then, a causal conclusion stays tentative and open to new evidence. In the video, picking names from a hat stands for random assignment, and the socks stand for an exposure. Sources: K. J. Rothman, Epidemiology: An Introduction, 3rd ed., Chapter 4 ('The Counterfactual Ideal'); T. L. Lash, T. J. VanderWeele, S. Haneuse and K. J. Rothman, Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., Chapters 3 and 5.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up to help you fold a paper plane.
  2. Stand behind a line and throw it five times, the same way, from the same spot. Mark where it lands each time. Throw only into an open space, never towards anyone.
  3. Now put a sticker on the same plane and throw it five more times, the same way, from the same spot. Mark where it lands.
  4. Did the plane fly further with the sticker, or about the same? Why is it fairer to test the same plane both ways?

In late August 1854 a severe outbreak of cholera began in Soho, London; the London Museum counts about 500 deaths in ten days. Cholera causes severe diarrhoea, so the body loses water fast. At the time most experts believed diseases like cholera spread through 'miasma', bad air from rotting matter, and the germ theory of disease was not yet accepted. John Snow (1813-1858), a London doctor who was also a pioneer of anaesthesia, had argued for several years that cholera was spread by drinking water contaminated with sewage. He found that the deaths clustered around the public pump in Broad Street and that most of the people who died had drunk its water, and he persuaded the Board of Guardians of St James's parish to remove the pump handle (8 September 1854). As Rothman notes, the outbreak was already waning when the handle came off (Snow's own daily counts of fatal attacks are drawn in the video), so it is not clear how many cases the removal prevented, though it may have prevented a second wave. Later investigation showed that the brickwork around the pump was defective and that water from a cesspool next to it could enter the pump's well. Snow's most important study was what he called an experiment 'on the grandest scale': in parts of south London two companies piped water down the same streets, the Southwark and Vauxhall Company with Thames water contaminated by London's sewage and the Lambeth Company, which in 1852 had moved its intake upstream to Thames Ditton. Households differed in water supply but not in wealth or occupation, often without knowing which company supplied them. In 1854 the risk of fatal cholera was about 1.6% among Southwark and Vauxhall customers and 0.27% among Lambeth customers, about six times higher (Rothman, Table 5.1); these figures cover all of the two companies' customers in London, not only the streets where their pipes were intermixed. The video says 'cholera struck' for these fatal attacks. Studies like this, where circumstances create the comparison an experimenter would have made, are called natural experiments. Snow's work did not convince most of the medical establishment at the time, but today he is considered the founding father of epidemiology (and of anaesthesiology). In the video the map with dots stands for the information Snow collected (his famous published map was drawn a little later). Sources: K. J. Rothman, Epidemiology: An Introduction, 3rd ed., Chapters 2 and 5; T. L. Lash, T. J. VanderWeele, S. Haneuse and K. J. Rothman, Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., Chapters 1 and 6; London Museum; LSHTM podcast (March 2013).

Why can't we take big bottles or sharp things on a plane?

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Safe bags make safe flights!

At the airport every cabin bag goes through an X-ray scanner. X-rays pass through the bag and are caught by a detector on the other side; dense materials such as metal absorb more of them, so they show up dark, while soft things such as clothes, paper and food let more through. Modern scanners use two X-ray energies to estimate what each object is made of and colour the picture: organic materials appear orange, metal and glass blue or green, and denser things darker (BBC Science Focus). Sharp objects such as knives and pointed metal scissors are not allowed in the cabin, because they could injure people in a confined space; they travel in checked baggage instead. Liquids are harder: on an X-ray picture many different liquids look very similar, and a few liquids are hazardous or flammable. Since 2007, following ICAO guidelines that most countries adopted, liquids, aerosols and gels in cabin baggage must be in containers of 100 ml or less, all in one clear, re-sealable bag of about 1 litre (the limit is the size of the container, so a big bottle that is only partly full is still not allowed); medicines and baby food are exempt (Airports Authority of India security information; Air India). Limiting the amount keeps any risk small, and the clear bag lets officers check the containers quickly. Newer 3D (CT) scanners can analyse liquids better, and some airports that have them, for example several UK airports in 2026, allow containers of up to 2 litres; rules differ between airports and countries, so check before you fly. In India the 100 ml rule applies. The torch activity is only an analogy: visible light passes through fewer things than X-rays do. Sources: Airports Authority of India, airport security information; Air India cabin baggage guidelines; ICAO guidelines on liquids, aerosols and gels (UN News, December 2006); BBC Science Focus, 'How does an airport scanner work?'; Which?, 'The UK's airport liquid rules for 2026'.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for a torch, a sheet of paper, a clear plastic bottle of water and a metal spoon.
  2. Make the room dark and shine the torch at a plain wall. Never shine it into anyone's eyes.
  3. Hold each thing, one at a time, between the torch and the wall. Look at the wall: a bright patch, a dim glow, or a dark shadow?
  4. Which things let the light through, and which block it? The X-ray scanner at the airport works a bit like this, but X-rays can pass through much more than light can.

Why do we burp?

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Air goes in, burps come out!

A burp is just gas leaving the stomach. Whenever we eat or drink we swallow some air with the food, and eating or drinking too fast, or drinking through a straw, sends extra air down. Fizzy drinks add even more gas: their fizz is carbon dioxide. The gas collects at the top of the stomach, and the extra gas is pushed back up the esophagus (the food tube) and out of the mouth. The noise comes from a ring of muscle at the top of the esophagus: the gas pushes through it and makes the throat vibrate, a bit like air squeaking out of a stretched balloon neck. Grown-ups pat a baby's back to help the burp come out, so the extra gas does not make the baby uncomfortable. Burping is normal. Slow sips mean fewer burps, and the polite thing is to cover your mouth and say excuse me. Sources: KidsHealth (Nemours), "Why Do I Burp?"; The Conversation, Curious Kids, "Why do burps make noise?".

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up to blow up a balloon and hold its neck closed. Balloons only with a grown-up: if a balloon pops, give all the pieces to a grown-up. Never put balloon pieces in your mouth.
  2. Stretch the neck sideways with your fingers, so it makes a thin slit.
  3. Let the air squeak out slowly. Pull the neck tighter or looser: does the sound change?
  4. Talk about it: how is the squeaky balloon neck like the top of your food tube when you burp?

Why does popcorn pop?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Steam inside makes it pop!

A popcorn kernel is a seed with a hard, almost waterproof outer shell (the hull, or pericarp) around a store of starch, and about 14% of it is water. When the kernel is heated, that water turns into steam. Steam takes up far more room than liquid water, and because the hull traps it, the pressure inside rises. At about 180 °C (much hotter than boiling water) the hull can no longer hold: it cracks, the steam rushes out, and the hot, softened (gelatinised) starch puffs out and sets into the white, fluffy flake. High-speed films by Emmanuel Virot and Alexandre Ponomarenko (2015) showed that the pop sound comes from the steam escaping, not from the hull cracking, and that a bulge of starch pushes against the pan like a little leg and makes the popcorn jump. A kernel with a cracked hull lets the steam leak out, and a kernel that has dried out has too little water, so neither builds enough pressure to pop well. The oldest popcorn found so far, from Paredones and Huaca Prieta on the north coast of Peru, is about 6,700 years old. Popping needs a hot pan, so it is a grown-up's job, with a lid on the pan. The paper-bag activity shows a related idea without heat: clapping squeezes the trapped air, and the squeezed air pushes on the paper until it bursts. Sources: Science Buddies, 'Explore the POP in popcorn'; E. Virot and A. Ponomarenko, Journal of the Royal Society Interface (2015), reported in Science News Explores, 'How popcorn got its pop'; Guinness World Records, 'Earliest popcorn'.

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Ask a grown-up for a paper bag. Never use a plastic bag.
  2. Blow into the bag with a few big breaths (rest if you feel dizzy), then twist the top shut so the air can't get out.
  3. Hold the bag away from everyone's faces and clap your hands on it hard. Pop! It's loud, so warn everyone first.
  4. What burst the bag? Your clap squeezed the air inside, and the squeezed air pushed on the paper until it burst. In a popcorn kernel, heat turns the water into steam, and the steam pushes on the shell until it bursts.

How does a caterpillar become a butterfly?

Watch the story first, then tap Try it and do the science yourself.

Turn the phone sideways for a bigger picture

Watch on YouTube
Curie's big idea
Egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly!

Butterflies change completely as they grow. A butterfly lays tiny eggs on the plant its caterpillars will eat. A caterpillar (the larva) hatches, and a monarch caterpillar's first meal is its own eggshell; then it eats leaves and grows fast: a monarch caterpillar gets about 2,000 times heavier in about two weeks. Its skin cannot grow with it, so it sheds its skin several times (most caterpillars shed five times in all). The last time it sheds, a chrysalis (the pupa) is underneath. Inside, the caterpillar's body breaks down and is rebuilt into a butterfly, with wings, legs and antennae. This complete change of body is called metamorphosis. The butterfly comes out with wet, crumpled wings, pumps them up, lets them dry and flies away, and later lays eggs of its own. Sources: American Museum of Natural History, A guide to the butterfly life cycle; Butterfly Conservation, Life cycle of butterflies and moths; Journey North (monarch caterpillar growth).

Try it at home
👋 with a grown-up
  1. Go on a leaf hunt with a grown-up, in a garden or a park.
  2. Ask the grown-up to gently turn over some leaves, and look underneath for tiny round eggs. Look, but don't touch!
  3. Look for leaves with nibbled holes or bitten edges: a caterpillar may be eating nearby. Wash your hands when you get home.
  4. Talk about it: what will a caterpillar become? What comes before, and after?
